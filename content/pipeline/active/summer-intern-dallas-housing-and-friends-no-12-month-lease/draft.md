*By Jadesse · 7 min read*

You got the internship. Congratulations, genuinely. Then you opened a map of Dallas, typed your office address into an apartment search, and watched the panic set in.

Ten weeks. No lease that makes sense. Maybe no car. Definitely no friends yet.

I've been the person staring at a "Dallas Apts" folder in my bookmarks bar, trying to figure out a city I didn't live in yet. So I want to talk through the three things that actually keep summer interns up at night, because the internet keeps treating them like separate problems when they're really one.

## The real problem isn't housing. It's three problems wearing a trench coat.

Search "summer intern housing Dallas" and you'll get a wall of furnished-room aggregators. Search "how to make friends in a new city" and you'll get a completely different set of articles that act like you have all the time in the world.

You don't. You have ten weeks, and the clock starts the day you land.

So the panic comes in three flavors. Where do I sleep that won't lock me into a year? How do I get to work without buying a car for a summer? And who am I going to eat dinner with so I'm not spending Friday night alone in a stranger's spare room?

The mistake is solving them one at a time. The fix is choosing housing that quietly answers all three at once.

## 1. You do not need a 12-month lease (and most won't even offer you one)

This is the part that trips people up first. Standard Dallas apartments want a 12-month commitment, sometimes 14. Some won't go below that no matter how nicely you ask.

For a summer, you have better options:

- **Furnished short-term sublets** (think 2 to 4 months) listed on the usual intern-housing sites. Furnished is non-negotiable here. You are not shipping a bed for ten weeks.
- **University summer housing** at UTD, TCU, or UTA, which open dorm and apartment-style rooms to interns in summer. Cheaper, but often quieter and further from the office.
- **Furnished coliving**, where the room comes ready and the lease is built around shorter stays.

Whatever you pick, ask two questions before you sign anything: is the full term covered (no awkward "the place is only available through July 20" gaps), and what is actually included. A room that looks cheap but needs you to buy a mattress, find wifi, and set up utilities isn't cheap. It's a part-time job.

## 2. Sort out getting around before you sign, not after

The Dallas car question is real, and the honest answer is: it depends entirely on where you sleep.

Dallas is spread out. DART (the light rail and bus system) covers a lot of the core, including downtown, Uptown, and a chunk of the northern suburbs, but coverage gets thin fast once you're off the rail lines. A spot that's a ten-minute drive from your office can be a fifty-minute, two-transfer trip if it's not near a station.

So flip the order. Don't find housing and then figure out the commute. Find your office, look at what's near a DART line within reach of it, and house-hunt inside that circle.

If you'll be carless, a place within walking distance of a DART station plus the occasional rideshare usually beats buying or renting a car for a summer. Map your real morning commute before you commit, on a weekday, at the actual hour you'd leave. The "it's fine" you feel looking at a map at 11pm is not the same as standing on a platform at 7:45am.

## 3. The loneliness is the part nobody plans for

Here's what surprised me most about being somewhere new: the logistics are solvable in a weekend. The loneliness is the thing that follows you to week six.

You can do everything right, furnished room, easy commute, and still come home every night to silence. Internships are short enough that it's genuinely hard to build a social life from scratch. By the time you've found your people, you're packing to leave.

A few things that actually help:

- **Say yes to the intern cohort.** Your company almost certainly has other summer interns. Awkward group lunches now become your Saturday plans by July.
- **Pick housing where community is the default, not a project.** If you're already living with people in the same season of life, you skip the hardest part: the cold start.
- **Lower the bar for "friends."** You're not looking for lifelong best friends in ten weeks. You're looking for someone to get tacos with on a Tuesday. That's enough, and it's a lot.

Where you live decides how hard this is. A random roommate off a listing might be lovely or might be a stranger you nod at in the kitchen. The structure of the place matters more than people think.
