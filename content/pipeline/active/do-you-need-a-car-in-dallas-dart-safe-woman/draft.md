*By Jadesse · 7 min read*

It's your second week in Dallas and you're standing in a parking lot the size of a small country, holding two grocery bags and your phone, watching the rideshare estimate climb while the heat comes up off the asphalt in waves.

That's the third ride today. You do the quiet math. *At this rate, maybe I just buy the car.*

Then you open the other tab, the one with the insurance quote, and your stomach drops a little. So you close both and tell yourself you'll figure it out this weekend, which is what you told yourself last weekend.

If you're trying to decide whether to ship a car, buy one, or risk going without, here's the honest version nobody puts in the relocation listicles.

## The short answer: eventually, probably. On day one, no.

Dallas is a car city. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. The metro is enormous, a lot of it was built around the highway, and over a long enough timeline most people here end up with a car.

But "eventually" and "before I even know the city" are two completely different decisions, and the listicles love to collapse them into one scary yes.

You do not need to arrive with a car. You especially don't need to buy one in your first month, sight unseen, while you're also paying to move and figuring out which neighborhood is even yours. The expensive mistake isn't going car-free. It's locking into a car payment and a year of insurance before you've learned how you actually move through this city.

## What a car really costs here in 2026

This is the part that changed recently, and it's worth saying out loud.

Dallas car insurance is running around $3,685 a year for the average driver in 2026, and steeper than that in some ZIP codes. That's before the car payment. Before gas. Before you've paid for a single parking spot or circled a block looking for one.

Stack it up and a car is easily one of the largest line items in your first year here, right next to rent. When you're already absorbing the cost of a move, that's a lot of money to commit to something you're not even sure you need yet.

So the real question isn't "is Dallas walkable enough." It's "can I land somewhere that buys me time before I take on that bill." Usually, yes.

## DART is better than its reputation

Dallas has the largest light rail system in the country by track length, and it just got bigger. The Silver Line opened in late 2025, adding 26 miles across the northern suburbs and, crucially, a rail connection toward DFW Airport. That last part matters more than it sounds when you're new here and flying home for holidays. No airport parking, no surge-priced ride at 5am.

If you land in one of the walkable cores, Uptown, Knox-Henderson, downtown, parts of Bishop Arts and Lower Greenville, you can do a lot on foot and on rail. Uptown alone scores in the low 90s on Walk Score. The grocery run, the gym, the coffee, the train, all of it within reach without a steering wheel.

Where DART gets thin is the last mile, the gap between the station and your door. Dallas built GoLink for exactly that: an on-demand ride inside your transit zone that connects you to the rest of the system, booked through the app and covered as part of the fare. It's the piece that makes car-free actually workable instead of theoretical.

None of this makes Dallas New York. It makes Dallas a place where, in the right neighborhood, you can comfortably go without a car for your first stretch here. And your first stretch is exactly when you most want to keep your options open.

## "But is DART safe for a woman alone at night?"

Here's the question underneath all the others, the one the transit maps never answer.

You're not really asking whether the train exists. You're asking whether car-free secretly means trapped at home after dark, or paying for a ride every single night because the alternative doesn't feel safe.

I'll be straight with you, because the internet won't. The honest answer from women who ride it is: it depends on the line, the station, and the hour. Daytime and commute hours on the busier lines feel fine. Late nights, emptier platforms, and long waits between trains are where it starts to feel different, and recent service cuts have made some of those off-peak waits longer than they used to be.

So the move isn't blind faith in the system. It's having a plan. Know which lines and stations feel right to you. Know when to switch from the train to GoLink or a quick rideshare for the last leg. And, most of all, know that you don't have to be solving this alone at 11pm on a platform with one bar of signal.

That last part is the one that actually changes the math.

## Where you live decides almost all of this

Notice how every honest answer keeps circling back to the same variable. Not the car. Not the train. *Where you start from.*

A car-free life in Dallas is genuinely hard if you land in a far-flung apartment off a six-lane road, where the nearest anything is a fifteen-minute drive and the parking garage is the scariest part of your night. It's genuinely easy if you land somewhere walkable, near transit, with people around who already know how the buses and trains actually work.

That's the piece the listicles can't sell you, because it isn't a Walk Score. It's who's home when you get there.

I'll be honest about my bias, because Dali House exists for this exact woman. It's a coliving home in Dallas for women who are relocating, starting over, or stepping into whatever's next, and it's sited so that car-free is realistic instead of aspirational. Walkable surroundings, transit you can reach, and housemates who've already figured out the grocery run and the late-night ride home.

The safety question gets a lot smaller when you're not facing it solo. You text the group chat that you're ten minutes out. Someone's awake. You split the rideshare home from the concert because three of you went together. You borrow the housemate's car for the big Costco trip and skip owning one entirely for a while. The thing that makes car-free feel risky, doing it completely alone, is the thing a house full of women quietly removes.

## A simple plan for your first 90 days car-free

If you want to test car-free before you commit, here's the version that actually works instead of the version that leaves you stranded.

**Land walkable on purpose.** Before anything else, choose a starting address where your daily basics, groceries, gym, coffee, a train, are reachable without a steering wheel. This one choice does more than any app. Get it right and the rest is easy. Get it wrong and no amount of planning saves you.

**Set up the apps before you need them.** Put the DART GoPass app and GoLink on your phone in week one, not in a panic on a dark platform. Take one daytime practice ride to the places you'll actually go, so the system stops being abstract.

**Budget honestly for rides.** Even car-free, plan for some rideshare, usually a couple hundred dollars a month for the trips transit doesn't cover. That's still a fraction of a car payment plus that $3,685 insurance bill, and it flexes down as you learn the routes.

**Have a night plan, not a night gamble.** Decide in advance how you get home after dark: which lines feel right, when to switch to GoLink or a rideshare, and who you can text that you're on your way. The plan is what turns "is this safe" into "I've got this."

## A soft landing doesn't require a car payment in week one

So, do you need a car in Dallas?

Someday, maybe. Probably. But not to arrive, and not to be okay while you find your footing. The costly version of this move is the one where you panic-buy a car in month one because your apartment left you stranded. The soft version is the one where you land somewhere walkable, with people and a plan, and let the city teach you what you actually need before you spend the money.

Give yourself the second version. Keep your options open, keep your first year lighter, and decide about the car once Dallas is a place you know instead of a parking lot you're standing in.

The car can wait. The landing is the part worth getting right.
