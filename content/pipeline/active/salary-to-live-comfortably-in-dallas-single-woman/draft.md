*By Jadesse · 7 min read*

It's almost midnight and you're three tabs deep into a salary calculator when the headline stops you cold.

*You need to make almost six figures to live comfortably in Dallas.*

You read it twice. You look at your own number, the one on your actual offer letter, and it's nowhere near that. Something in your chest tightens. *Maybe I can't afford this after all. Maybe I should just stay.*

Close the tab. Before you talk yourself out of the whole move, let me show you what that headline is quietly leaving out, because I almost let a number just like it decide my life for me.

## Where the scary number comes from

You didn't imagine it. A widely shared 2026 study put the salary to live "comfortably" in Dallas up near the high nineties, and local news ran with it, because a six-figure headline travels. It got reshared into every feed until it started to feel like a fact about you instead of an estimate.

Here's the thing about that word, comfortably. In these studies it's a formula, usually some version of the 50/30/20 rule: half your income on needs, a third on wants, the rest to savings and debt. It's a fine budgeting ideal. It is not the line between surviving and not surviving. It's the line between "comfortable by a textbook definition" and "doing fine but watching your spending," which is how most of us actually live, in every city.

So the number isn't fake. It's just answering a different question than the one you're scared about. You're asking *can I afford to move here.* The headline is answering *what salary makes a budgeting spreadsheet look effortless.* Those are not the same question.

## What the headline quietly assumes

Every one of these comfortable-salary numbers is built on a stack of assumptions, and almost all of them describe one specific person: a woman renting a one-bedroom apartment by herself and paying for everything alone.

Look at what's baked in. A solo one-bedroom, which in the walkable neighborhoods runs somewhere around $1,500 to $1,900 a month. Every utility in your name. A full furnishing budget, because moving alone means no roommate's couch and no family garage to raid. Renters insurance, internet, the works, none of it split with anyone.

That's the most expensive way a person can possibly house themselves in this city. The headline takes the priciest version of the move and turns it into the price of admission. Of course the number comes out high. It was built high.

Change the housing assumption and the whole figure moves. That's not a trick. It's just the biggest line in the budget doing what big lines do.

## The real floor is a lot lower than the headline

If you want the honest bottom of the range instead of the comfortable top, look at the living-wage research, the kind that calculates what it actually takes to cover your needs in Dallas. That number lands far closer to the mid-forties than to a hundred thousand.

I'm not telling you to aim for the bare minimum. Nobody wants to white-knuckle their budget. I'm telling you the truth is a wide range, and the internet handed you the scariest end of it and called it the requirement.

Plenty of women live full, good lives in Dallas on salaries in the fifties and sixties. They are not suffering. They're just not the person the comfortable-salary headline was modeling, because that person was paying for an entire apartment by herself.

## The number nobody actually adds up

While we're being honest about money, let's talk about the part that genuinely catches people off guard, and it isn't the monthly rent.

It's the lump sum you need before you can live anywhere at all. First month, a security deposit, the application and admin fees you pay per applicant whether or not you're approved, the moving costs, and then furnishing a place from nothing.

Add it up and the move-in stack alone can run several thousand dollars before you've slept one night. Furnishing a first apartment from scratch lands anywhere from around fifteen hundred for a studio to several thousand for a one-bedroom, and that's months of hauling things home before the place even feels finished.

That's the number that actually stops people. Not "can I make rent," but "can I get in the door at all." And it's the one the salary headlines never show you, because it doesn't fit in a tidy annual figure.

## A quick gut-check on your own number

If you want to replace the scary headline with something honest about your situation, run these four questions instead of the viral one.

**What's my all-in housing number, not just rent?** Add utilities, internet, and renters insurance to the rent, because a solo apartment unbundles all of it. This is the figure that actually shapes your month, and it's the one a furnished, bundled room collapses into a single line.

**What's my real move-in cash, today?** Total the deposit, first month, per-applicant fees, moving costs, and furnishing. That lump sum, not the annual salary, is what usually decides whether you can move now or in three months.

**Am I pricing the solo version by default?** If every number you've written down assumes you alone in your own apartment, you're modeling the most expensive path without meaning to. Ask what changes if the housing isn't solo.

**Is this a comfortable number or a survival number?** Be clear which one is scaring you. "Not textbook-comfortable yet" is a very different problem than "can't make it work," and most people confuse the two at midnight.

Answer those four and the six-figure headline loses most of its power, because you're finally pricing your move instead of someone else's spreadsheet.

## You can afford the move. Just not the expensive way.

I almost let a salary number make a much bigger decision for me once, and I'm glad I didn't. So here's what I'd tell the version of you with eleven tabs open at midnight.

You don't need six figures to move to Dallas. You need a clear-eyed look at what that headline was secretly assuming, and a housing choice that doesn't quietly sign you up for the priciest version of everything.

The move is more affordable than the internet made it sound. It just asks you to be a little smarter about how you land, especially in the first year, when the upfront costs hit hardest and your network is smallest.

Don't let a number built for someone else's spreadsheet talk you out of your own next chapter. Land soft, keep your first year light, and let the city prove to you that you could afford it all along.
