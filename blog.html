What No One Tells You About Relocating to Dallas as a Woman

April 14, 2025 · By Jadesse · 5 min read

Moving to a new city alone is one of the most quietly disorienting experiences a person can go through. You pack your whole life into boxes, drive toward something new, and then spend the first few weeks wondering why you feel so untethered — even when everything is technically going fine.

I moved back to Dallas after several years away. I thought coming back would feel easy. I grew up here. I knew the roads, the neighborhoods, the heat. But knowing a place as a child is very different from building a life there as an adult. The city felt simultaneously familiar and foreign, and I spent longer than I'd like to admit feeling like I was just passing through my own life.

"The hardest part of relocating isn't the logistics. It's the quiet."

The logistics are the easy part

Everyone warns you about the practical stuff. Find an apartment. Set up utilities. Get your Texas driver's license (yes, you actually have to do this). And honestly? That part is manageable. There are checklists for it. There's Google.

What nobody warns you about is the social infrastructure you left behind. The coworker you'd grab lunch with. The friend who'd text you about a random dinner on a Tuesday. The gym where you knew the regulars. All of that disappears overnight, and rebuilding it — especially as a woman in her 20s or 30s — takes far longer than anyone admits.

Why it's harder for women

There's a particular challenge that doesn't get discussed enough: meeting people as an adult woman in a new city can feel genuinely unsafe, or at least deeply uncomfortable. You're navigating who to trust, which spaces feel right, and how to build something real without burning yourself out trying.

  • Bars and networking events rarely lead to genuine friendships
  • Apps like Bumble BFF exist but feel transactional
  • Work colleagues are a start, but rarely fill the whole gap
  • The gym, coffee shops, and classes help — but take time

The missing piece, for most women I've talked to, is proximity. Living near people who are going through the same thing, who share similar values, and who you see regularly enough that connection can grow organically.

What actually helped

For me, the turning point wasn't a single event or app. It was changing my environment. When you live with people who are thoughtfully chosen, who are also building their lives, community doesn't feel like a project — it just happens. You run into each other in the kitchen. You ask about each other's weeks. You notice when something's off.

That's the core idea behind Dali House. Not a program. Not a forced social calendar. Just a well-designed environment that makes connection possible — and a host who has been through exactly what you're going through.

What Is Coliving — And Is It Right for You?

March 28, 2025 · By Jadesse · 4 min read

The word "coliving" gets thrown around a lot, and it means different things to different people. For some it conjures images of startup bro houses with ping-pong tables. For others it sounds like a glorified hostel. Neither of those is what Dali House is — so let's clear a few things up.

What coliving actually means

At its simplest, coliving is intentional shared housing. You have your own private bedroom. You share common spaces — kitchen, living room, bathrooms — with a small number of housemates who have been selected with care. Everything you need is included: furniture, utilities, cleaning, supplies.

The key word is intentional. The difference between coliving and a random roommate situation is that someone — the host or operator — has designed the experience. They've thought about who lives there, how the space is set up, and what kind of culture they want to create.

Who coliving is for

Coliving tends to work best for people in one of a few situations:

  • New to a city and want to start with community built in
  • Between apartments and need a flexible, furnished option
  • Working long hours and want home to be effortless
  • Value connection but don't want the chaos of a typical roommate setup

It's not for everyone. If you strongly prefer total privacy, or if you work from home and need complete quiet, a solo apartment might suit you better. But if you've ever finished a long day and wished there was someone to debrief with — someone who gets it — coliving might be exactly what you're missing.

Why Community Is the Missing Piece in Your Relocation Plan

March 10, 2025 · By Jadesse · 4 min read

Most relocation checklists look something like this: find housing, update your address, set up a new bank account, find a doctor, maybe join a gym. Practical stuff. Important stuff. But there's one thing almost nobody puts on the list: build community.

And it shows. Studies consistently find that loneliness peaks in early adulthood, particularly during life transitions like moves, career changes, or breakups. The structure that made friendships easy — school, dorms, shared workplaces — disappears, and most people are surprised by how hard it is to replace.

"Belonging doesn't happen by accident. It happens by proximity, repetition, and shared context."

Why the usual advice doesn't work

"Just put yourself out there" is the most common advice, and it's not wrong, but it's incomplete. The research on friendship suggests that three things drive connection: proximity (being near people), repeated unplanned interactions, and a setting that encourages openness. Most adult social environments — bars, networking events, fitness classes — only reliably deliver one of the three.

Environment as infrastructure

The most reliable way to build community isn't to try harder. It's to change your environment. When you live with people who share your values and your season of life, the repeated unplanned interactions happen automatically. You build context over time. Trust forms without effort.

That's the simple premise behind Dali House. A thoughtfully curated environment where community isn't a program — it's just what happens when the right people share a home.

Moving to Dallas–Fort Worth: A Practical First Look

February 22, 2025 · By Jadesse · 3 min read

Dallas–Fort Worth is the fourth-largest metro in the United States and one of the fastest-growing. If you're relocating here for work, to be closer to family, or just for a change of pace, you'll find a city that is sprawling, affordable relative to coastal metros, and genuinely full of things to do — once you figure out where everything is.

The basics

DFW is big. Like, really big. The metro covers over 9,000 square miles and has over 7 million residents. This means that "proximity" works differently here than it does in denser cities. You'll probably want a car. DART (the light rail) covers some ground but is nowhere near comprehensive enough to go car-free for most lifestyles.

Neighborhoods worth knowing

  • Uptown — walkable, restaurant-dense, young professionals
  • Deep Ellum — music, art, nightlife, eclectic energy
  • Bishop Arts — indie shops, coffee, quieter version of Uptown
  • Carrollton / Plano — suburban, more space, easy access to major highways
  • Frisco / Allen — newer development, family-oriented, tech-heavy employers

Cost of living

Relative to New York, LA, or Seattle, Dallas is significantly more affordable. Rent for a one-bedroom apartment in a desirable area typically runs $1,400–$2,200/month. No state income tax is a meaningful bonus. Food, entertainment, and services are all reasonably priced.
